about the paper

Cyanotype was worked out in 1842: paper brushed with two iron salts, ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide, dried in the dark. Ultraviolet light turns the iron where it falls, and the turned part, washed in water, stays as Prussian blue; the rest washes away and leaves the paper white. A year later a botanist began printing seaweeds this way, laid straight on the paper, and made a book of them, the first book illustrated by any photographic process.

Things printed in contact like this are photograms: no camera, only shadow. Flat things printed under glass come out sharp. Things that stand up off the paper, a key on its bow, a bottle on its side, a feather that curls, let the open sky in under their edges, so they print soft, and if the sun moves while the paper is out, their shadows move with it and the edges go double.

While it's out, the exposed paper goes from yellow-green to slate and then to a dull bronze; that bronze is how printers judged it, by eye. Too short and the blue washes out pale. Too long and the light creeps in under everything. Fresh from the wash the blue is light and a little green; it deepens as it dries and the iron oxidises in the air.

The sky here is a model: the sun's height and bearing are worked out for each minute at latitude 52° north, ultraviolet falls off steeply when the sun is low, and cloud takes most of the direct light but brightens the sky a little.
