A lock is a box of water with a gate at each end. To go up, you bring the boat into an empty chamber, shut the gate behind her, and let water in from the pound above until the levels meet; then the top gate will open, and not before, because a few centimetres of head hold a gate shut better than any lock-keeper. To go down, the same backwards.

Tap a gate to choose it. A gate has its paddles, the sluices that let water through it, and the gate itself. Draw the paddle and wait; when the water is level either side, the gate can be opened. Tap a boat to choose her, then send her up or down through an open gate.

Water

Every chamber you fill going up, and every one you empty coming down, passes a lockful of water one step down the hill. All of it comes from the summit in the end, and the summit is fed by a reservoir that may be low by August. So the meter that matters is the one at the top: how many lockfuls this flight has drawn from the summit pound.

The cheapest thing in the world is a lock that is set for you. A boat coming up into a chamber that a boat going down has just left empty pays nothing to get in, and leaves the chamber full for whoever is coming the other way. Boaters call it the lock being with you. Turn a lock round for nobody and you've sent a lockful down to the sea.

Short pounds

Between the locks of a flight the pounds are sometimes hardly longer than a boat. Filling a chamber out of one draws it down by more than it can spare, and a boat in it sits on the mud. The answer is to let the lock above you down at the same time, so the water you take out of the pound is put back as fast as you take it. If you're out of step the other way, the pound fills to its weir and the surplus goes down the bywash, round the lock and away.

Staircases

In a staircase there are no pounds at all. Each chamber's top gate is the next one's bottom gate, and the only place a chamber can get its water from is the chamber above it. Going up, the chamber above must be full before you can fill yours from it, and that one must have been filled from above. Get it the wrong way round, and let a full chamber into another full one, and the water comes over the coping and down the towpath.

About the model

Every chamber here holds one boat and rises two metres; the pounds and the summit and the bottom of the flight are wide enough to move only a little, or not at all. Water passes a drawn paddle at a rate that goes as the square root of the head across it, and through an open gate almost at once. A pound that rises past its weir spills down a bywash to the next pound below. A boat draws seventy centimetres and goes aground with less than seventy-five under her. Nothing here is a real flight, though all of it happens on real ones.
