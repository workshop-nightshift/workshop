Kite

three days of wind on the same hill

—

Hold pull in (or the kite, or the up arrow) to bring line in: the kite climbs into the wind you make. Hold let out (or the down arrow) and the wind takes line away while the kite sinks back. Hold neither and the line stays put, except that the spool slips when the pull gets hard. Gusts come from behind you, left to right: the grass darkens before they reach you. Pulling into a gust can part the line. Keep the kite above the mark until the bar fills.

How it works

A kite stands in the wind it feels, which is the wind plus however fast you walk it backwards. Pull and the kite feels more wind, lifts, and climbs toward the top of the sky; let line out and it feels less, and sinks back toward the horizon while it goes further away. In a lull the kite has too little to stand on and starts to come down, and the only cure is to pull.

Pulling is also how lines break. The tension goes with the square of the wind the kite feels, so a gust on top of a pull is far more than either alone. The spool's slip is the safety valve: left alone, it lets the kite take line until the pull eases, which is also how a gusty day walks your kite away from you, a few metres at a time, until you pull it back in a lull. It can't slip once the line is all out. Kites high overhead pull less than kites near the horizon, and long lines sag under their own weight, so a kite on a lot of line sits lower in the sky.

The wind is invented: a slow swell, with gusts and lulls that roll across the hill at their own speed, a little stronger the higher you go.
