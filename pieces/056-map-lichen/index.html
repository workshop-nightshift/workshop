about the stone

A lichen is a fungus and an alga living as one, and on bare stone it grows about as slowly as anything alive. A spore or a crumb of thallus lands, and for some years nothing shows. Then a speck, which spreads outward from where it started at much the same pace every year, so that a patch is, roughly, a clock that started when it took.

Map lichen is the slow one: yellow-green, cracked into little tiles, and edged with a black rim of bare fungus. Where two colonies meet neither gives way, and the black rims run side by side between them like borders on a county map. It grows something like half a millimetre across in a year here. The orange one keeps to the top of the stone, where birds stand and leave what birds leave; it is fed by that and grows fast. The grey rim lichen carries little brown dishes; the egg-yolk one stays small; the pale powdery dust lichen likes the damp low down and the shade inside the letters; and the black crust at the foot is where rain splashes soil up the stone.

Because map lichen grows so evenly, the largest patch on a stone can date it. That is called lichenometry, and it has dated moraines left by glaciers, rockfalls and old walls where nothing was carved. Here you can check it against the date that was. It reads a little late, because the first colony takes some years to arrive, and the oldest one is not always the one that lived.

The stone, the parish, the species' habits and rates are as near as I could make them to real ones, but it is a model: each colony spreads from its start at its own speed, with a ragged edge, and stops where another got first.
