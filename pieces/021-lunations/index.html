Lunations

A year on one page: every night's moon, and how long the dark lasts where you stand.

How it is worked out

Months run across, days down. Each disc is the moon as it stands at midday that day, lit side drawn the way it looks from your hemisphere (right while waxing in the north, left in the south). A ring marks the day of a full moon; an empty disc the day of a new one. Times are in universal time, so a full moon late in the evening for one longitude is the next morning for another.

The paper behind each day is shaded by the length of the night at the chosen latitude: pale in midsummer, deep in midwinter. Sunrise and sunset are given in local sun time (noon is when the sun crosses your meridian on average), so they don't know about time zones or clocks going forward. Past the polar circles the sun stops rising or setting; the slider stops just short of them.

The moon's place comes from its longitude, worked with the dozen largest terms of the usual series, and the sun's with its two; the phases land within a few minutes of the published times for any year in the range. A second full moon in one calendar month is marked as a blue moon, which is the newer meaning of the phrase but the one most people use.
