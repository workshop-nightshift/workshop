A field guide to weather that doesn't happen

Volume II, entries VI–X. Weather indoors, weather you hear, weather you smell. Volume I covered the sky; this one comes closer.

Entry VI

Hallway drizzle

pluvia transitus

Plate VI. First-floor landing, a house let in rooms.

A fine rain that falls in corridors and nowhere else. It never enters a room, and it stops at the top of the stairs as if at a doorstep. It is commonest in long houses that have been divided up, where many people pass each other on the way to somewhere and nobody stays in the hall.

It doesn't wet anything, exactly. Coats come in from it feeling a little heavier than they went out, and the carpet runner is always slightly darker down the middle, where people walk.

Season
Winter, when everyone is home but in separate rooms.
Signs
Footsteps outside your door that sound like they have somewhere to be.
Folklore
Stop and talk to a neighbour on the landing and it eases off for a week.
Dress
Slippers. Leave your door open a crack if you'd like it to pass.
Entry VII

Late thunder

tonitrus serotinus

Plate VII. A clear evening receiving a storm of some years back.

Thunder from a storm that has already finished, sometimes long ago. Sound is slow, and on certain still evenings the air seems to remember that it is a carrier, and delivers something it was holding. There is no flash and no cloud: only a low roll from a clear sky, coming from the direction the old storm stood.

Older people sometimes recognise it. "That's the August one," they say, and name a year, and nobody can prove them wrong.

Season
Clear evenings at the end of summer.
Signs
A dog that looks at the horizon before you hear anything.
Folklore
Count the seconds as you would for lightning, and the number is the years.
Dress
Whatever you were wearing then.
Entry VIII

Forescent

odor praeveniens

Plate VIII. Dry ground, smelling of next week.

The smell of rain on dry ground, arriving some days before the rain does. It is the same smell, earthy and slightly metallic, but there is nothing to account for it: the pavements are dry, the sky is high and clean, the forecast is fine for the week.

Gardeners trust it more than the forecast. The rain it promises is not always large, but it always comes, and it always comes to the same place the smell was strongest.

Season
Long dry spells, about the time people stop expecting a change.
Signs
Passers-by sniffing the air and looking puzzled.
Folklore
It is the ground practising.
Dress
Bring the washing in on Thursday.
Entry IX

Closed-room weather

caelum conclusum

Plate IX. Plan of a flat, back bedroom shut since spring.

Shut a room long enough and it begins to keep its own weather. At first it is only a change in the air when you open the door, cooler or warmer than the rest of the house. Leave it longer and it develops a sky: a small grey cloud turning near the ceiling, a light frost on the inside of the window in a month that has no frost.

It goes the moment the room is used again. Open the door, sit on the bed for an hour, and the cloud thins and settles as dust. What is left is the faint impression that the room had been getting on fine without you.

Season
Any. Commonest in rooms that belonged to someone who moved out.
Signs
A draught under a door with no window open behind it.
Folklore
Knock before going in; it is polite, and the weather has time to tidy itself.
Dress
A cardigan, kept on the hook by that door.
Entry X

Listening calm

tranquillitas auscultans

Plate X. A field going still; sounds arriving from three parishes away.

A calm so complete that sound travels further than it should. The wind drops, the grass stops, and then you begin to hear things from much too far away: a gate in the next valley, a spoon in a cup in a kitchen you can't see, two people on a distant road agreeing about something.

It lasts a few minutes. Anyone caught in it tends to go quiet themselves, from a sense that they are now audible to the same distances. When the wind returns, everyone talks a little louder than before, to make up for it.

Season
Evening, at the turn of the tide, inland or not.
Signs
Hearing your own pulse in an open field.
Folklore
Whatever you say in it will be heard by somebody, eventually, somewhere.
Dress
Nothing that rustles.
