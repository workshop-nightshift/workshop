Write the two refrains first. They need to rhyme with each other, and every stanza’s first line rhymes with them; every middle line shares a second rhyme. The refrains are put in for you.

nineteen lines, two rhymes

The villanelle began as a loose name for country songs in Italian and French; its fixed shape comes from one sixteenth-century poem by Jean Passerat, with English poets settling the rules later. Five tercets and a quatrain, nineteen lines, only two rhymes. The first and third lines are the refrains. They take turns closing the tercets, the first, then the second, then the first, and in the quatrain they come back one after the other as a couplet.

The threads on the left are the two refrains. Each dips out to the margin between its returns and they cross as they hand over, until the last two lines tie them. The small letters are the rhyme scheme: A¹ and A² are the refrains, a rhymes with them, b is the other sound.
