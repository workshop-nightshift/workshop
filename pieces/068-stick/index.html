Stick

Set a few lines of type by hand, justify them, pull a proof.

A composing stick holds a few lines of metal type. Each letter is a sort, a little block with its face cut backwards on the end, and the compositor takes them from their boxes in the case one by one and sets them in the stick upside down, left to right, nick outward, reading them as they go. The boxes are not the same size: the commonest letters sit in the biggest boxes, nearest the hand.

When a word won't fit the measure it goes on to the next line, and the line it left is justified: the word spaces are opened out with thinner and thinner pieces of blank metal (the thick space is a third of an em, the middle a quarter, the thin a fifth, the hair thinner still) until the line is tight and will lift without spilling. The last line of a paragraph is filled out with quads.

There are only so many of each letter in a fount. When the box is empty you are, as printers like to say, out of sorts. That probably isn't where the phrase comes from, but it is how it feels. Pull a proof, then distribute: every sort goes back to its own box, ready for the next job.

Notes

The measure here is seventeen ems, set solid, six lines to the stick. The lay of the case is this page's own, not a historical one, though like the old ones it gives the caps a third and the lower case the rest. The widths come from whatever serif your device has, so the same copy may break a little differently elsewhere. A few sorts in the fount are damaged; you'll see them in the proof when they come up.
