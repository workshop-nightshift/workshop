The gnomon is the triangle standing on the dial, and the edge that throws the shadow, the style, is not upright. It leans toward the celestial pole at exactly the latitude, so it lies parallel to the earth's axis. The sun then turns round it once a day at an even fifteen degrees an hour, whatever the season, and the shadow of that edge falls on the same line at the same hour all year. That is the whole trick, and the reason every dial is made for one latitude only.

Fifteen even degrees around a tilted edge do not land as fifteen even degrees on a flat stone. The hour lines crowd toward noon and spread toward morning and evening; the rule is that the tangent of a line's angle from noon is the sine of the latitude times the tangent of the hour. Move the latitude and watch them gather or fan. Near the equator the dial nearly folds flat; nearer the pole it opens to an even clock face.

The date lines are traced by the tip of the style: the summer solstice nearest the gnomon, the equinox a straight line across, the winter solstice farthest out. The shadow's tip rides one of them each day.

A sundial tells the sun's time, which runs fast and slow against a clock by up to a quarter of an hour over the year, because the earth's orbit is not a circle and its axis is tilted. Turn on the noon mark to see where the tip falls at twelve by a clock kept to the average sun: not a point but a figure eight, the analemma. Neither number here knows about time zones or summer time.
