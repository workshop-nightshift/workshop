the same broad pen, written faster: an italic hand that leans, narrows, and joins

the hand, letter by letter

Nib wrote a plain round hand: every bowl a circle, every arch a half of one, the pen lifted between letters. Chancery is what happens to that hand when it has to be written all day, quickly. The circles squeeze into ovals, the letters lean forward in the direction the hand is already travelling, and the arches stop sitting on top of their stems and spring out of them partway up, the pen climbing back up the stem it just came down before it turns away. That branching is most of what makes it look italic.

And the letters join. A pen that ends a letter with a small upward flick at the baseline is already heading for the next one; if the next one begins at the top left, as i, n, m, u and r do, it may as well keep going. The join is a hairline, because the pen is travelling nearly along its own edge. Letters that end at the top, like r, v, w and the bar of f, join across instead. Letters that begin at the top right, like a, o and d, are entered by lifting, as most scribes do. Turn the joins off to see the same skeletons lifted letter by letter, or show them to see where the pen ran on.

The hand was cut for the papal chancery in Rome and printed as a copybook in the 1520s; this is a loose version of it, drawn as skeletons and given to the same swept pen as Nib. The pen angle starts steeper here, about forty degrees, which is what puts the weight on the diagonals and keeps the joins fine. The capitals are plain, leaning, unflourished; the figures sit at a little under capital height.
