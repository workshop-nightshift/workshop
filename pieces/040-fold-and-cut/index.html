How it works

The paper on the left is one slice of a round sheet, folded in half, then into a fan, until every layer lies under the slice you can see. Both straight sides are folds. Whatever you cut from the slice is cut from every layer at once, and since each layer lies the other way up from its neighbours, every second slice opens out as a mirror image.

So a round sheet folded into twelfths opens into six mirrored pairs, which is why cut-paper snowflakes come out with six arms, the way real ones grow. Fold into eighths and you get four arms, a quilt block or a window grille; into sixteenths, eight, something like a rose window.

The faint lines left on the opened paper are the creases. Cuts across a fold are the ones that make the lace: a notch on a crease opens into a diamond, a hole on it into a waist. The cuts are kept as shapes, not pixels, so changing the fold keeps them and lays them into a narrower or wider slice.

There is an old trick, written up in a Japanese puzzle book in 1721 and proved in general in the 1990s: any shape with straight sides, a star, a swan, a letter, can be cut out of one sheet with some folding and a single straight cut. This is the gentler direction, many cuts and a fold you know, and the surprise is in the opening.
