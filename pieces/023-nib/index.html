the hand, letter by letter

about

A broad-edged pen has no idea which strokes are meant to be thick. Its edge is a short straight line held at one angle all the way through the writing. Where the pen travels across that edge the mark is as wide as the nib; where it travels along the edge the mark is a hairline. Everything in between is the angle doing its work.

So turn the pen. At thirty degrees the hand looks the way most people expect a written letter to look: heavy curves on the left and right of an o, thin at its top and bottom, the weight leaning a little. Flatten the pen to nothing and the uprights go fat and the joins go thin. Stand it upright and the whole thing turns over, every stem a hair and every crossbar a slab.

The nib width is given in nib-widths to the x-height, the way scribes measure: five nib-widths of the pen stacked up make the height of the small letters, at the default. Fewer is heavier. The letters are a plain round minuscule, drawn as their skeletons only; the pen makes the rest. There are no capitals yet.
