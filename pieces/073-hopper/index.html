about the salt

Water can hold only so much salt. Pour brine that is nearly full and let it dry, and the water leaves while the salt stays, so what is left grows stronger until it is more than full. Even then it waits a little, for something to start on. Then a grain appears, and the brine around it gives up its extra salt to it.

Common salt builds cubes. When the brine is only a little over full, the cube grows slowly and evenly and its faces stay flat. When it is far over, because the water is going fast, the corners and edges reach out into fresh brine and are fed first, and the middle of each face is starved. The edges run on ahead and leave the face behind as a hollow, then another inside that: a stepped, square pit like the hopper of a mill. Move the slate onto the stove and you get hoppers; leave it on a cold sill and you get small clear cubes.

The edge of a puddle dries first, and its edge stays put while the middle thins, so brine flows out towards the rim to replace what goes, carrying salt with it. That is why the crust and the first crystals gather in a ring. The last thin film goes all at once and leaves a frost of grains too small to have a shape.

Epsom salt holds on longer before it gives in, then shoots out in sprays of needles. Alum builds eight-sided crystals, and one lying on a face shows a triangle with its corners cut off.

This is a toy, not a model: the slate is a grid of a few thousand cells, each with a depth of water and a weight of salt, and the crystals are drawn, not grown molecule by molecule. But the two rules that matter are in it: salt starts only when the brine is well over full, and a crystal fed fast grows hollow.
