One Over Two

The sheep went through it in March. Twelve feet of wall to put back, a course at a time.

Pick a stone from the pile.

how it goes

A dry stone wall holds up with no mortar, only weight and the way the stones are crossed. The rule every waller is taught is one over two, two over one: each stone sits across the joint between two stones below it. Where a joint lands straight above another, the wall has a running joint, a seam that the frost, the sheep and the years will open.

You lay six courses, left to right, from the pile sorted for each: the big stones low, smaller ones as you go up. Tap a stone to try it in the wall, and the joint it would make is marked: red if it lands within a hand's width of a joint below. Tap it again to lay it. If it's too long for what's left, it gets dressed to fit; you can also knap any stone shorter, three inches at a blow. Leave a sliver at the end of a course and only a pinning stone will fill it, and pins work loose.

The fourth course is the through band. Through stones run the full width of the wall and tie its two faces together; you see only their ends. Space them not much more than three feet apart.

When the coping is on, let a hundred winters at it. A single running joint cracks. Two or more stacked one on another, and the wall drops into a gap there, the way the old one did. In a real wall each face is built like this, leaning in to a batter, with small stones packed into the middle as hearting; here you see one face. Today's pile is the same for everyone today.
