What make-ready is

Type is meant to stand exactly type-high, but it never quite does. Sorts wear down at the face, a line of furniture lifts a corner, the bed of an old press has a dip in it. Lock the forme up, pull a proof, and some letters print solid while others come off grey and broken, kissing the paper and no more.

The cure is make-ready. The printer marks the weak places on the proof, cuts pieces of thin tissue to the shape of a letter, a word or a line, and pastes them on the tympan, the packed sheet the paper lies against, exactly behind the weak spot. Each tissue is about a thousandth of an inch. Then another proof, and more marks. It can take longer than the run itself.

Two things tempt and don't help. More ink fills the grey in a little, but it floods the counters of every letter that was already right. More packing all over brings the weak sorts up, but drives the good ones into the paper until they bite and squash, ink pushed out to a rim round each stroke. Sometimes the answer is to take a sheet out and bring everything else up to meet the high spots.

Big letters need more pressure than small ones, as do rules and anything solid. Every new forme here has its own wear and its own uneven bed.
