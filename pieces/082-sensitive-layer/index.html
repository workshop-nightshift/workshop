Sensitive Layer

A cloud chamber: a box of alcohol mist over dry ice, where anything charged that passes through leaves a trail of droplets. Tap a trail to ask what made it.

Watch for a while. The short straight scratches are mostly muons, come down from the top of the sky. Thick stubby ones are alpha particles. Wandering threads are electrons.

How it works

A felt strip round the top of the box is soaked in isopropyl alcohol. The floor sits on a slab of dry ice, at about seventy-eight degrees below. Alcohol evaporates at the top, sinks, and cools as it falls, until just above the floor there is a layer of vapour colder than it ought to be while still vapour: supersaturated, ready to condense, with nothing yet to condense on.

A charged particle crossing that layer knocks electrons off the air molecules along its path. The ions it leaves are what the vapour has been waiting for. Droplets form on them within a fraction of a second, a line of mist marking where the particle went, and then fall, spread and fade. The layer is only a centimetre or two deep, so a muon coming steeply down crosses it in a short scratch; only one arriving nearly sideways draws a long line across the box.

The shape of a trail tells you what made it. Alphas are heavy and doubly charged: they ionise densely, go straight, and stop within a few centimetres of air. Muons are heavy but fast, and barely notice the air: thin, straight, and gone out of the layer. Electrons are light, and every molecule they pass nudges them, more so as they slow, so they wander. With a magnet under the floor, the electrons curl into hooks and spirals, all turning the same way; the alphas and muons, with far more momentum, go on as if it were not there.

The rates here are a little brisker than a real chamber's, and the shapes are drawn from the physics rather than measured. Thorium mantles are an old thing: most sold now have none. Low-sodium salt is mostly potassium chloride, and about one potassium atom in eight thousand is the radioactive kind.
