A shepherd's dial is a short wooden column with a turning cap, and a little blade folded into the cap. To read it you turn the cap until the blade stands over the day's mark, open the blade so it sticks out level, and hang the column from its cord so the blade points straight at the sun. The blade's shadow then falls straight down the column, and where its tip ends, among the curves drawn round the wood, is the hour.

A flat garden dial reads the sun's direction. This one reads the sun's height, which is why it doesn't need to know north and can live in a pocket. The shadow of a level blade of length g falls a depth g × tan(height) down the column: high sun, long shadow. So each hour gets a curve, deep in summer and shallow in winter, and the curves are what you see unrolled on the right.

The sun stands as high at ten as at two. The dial can't tell morning from afternoon; you have to know which half of the day it is, and the old dials label each curve with both hours. Near noon the curves run close together and flat, because the sun hardly climbs in its last hour up; that is where the dial is least sure of itself. Mid-morning and mid-afternoon, when the sun is climbing or falling quickly, it reads best.

Height depends on latitude, so a column is cut for one place. Recut this one further south and the summer noon sun climbs so steep that its shadow would run off the bottom of the wood; real dials simply lose those hours. Times are sun time, as a sundial knows it, with no zones or summer time.
