How it works

A tree puts on one ring a year, wide in a kind year and narrow in a hard one. Every oak in the vale lived through the same summers, so their rings rise and fall together, and a few years stand out in all of them: a late frost, a drought, the summer it never stopped raining. Those narrow years are the fingerprint.

The master chronology along the top is many trees averaged, overlapped back to 1450. The red line is one timber's core, its rings measured and smoothed the same way. Drag until the dips line up. The number is Student's t for the match at that position: anything under 3.5 is chance, a stray position can reach 5, and a true match here reads 8 or more. Scan does what the lab's computer does and tries every position at once.

A date for the rings is not yet a date for the building. Where the bark edge survives, the last ring is the summer before felling. Where the soft outer sapwood has worn away, the boundary with the heartwood still holds, and oak here carries fifteen to forty sapwood rings, so the felling falls in a window. Builders used oak green, within a year or so of felling. And they reused good timber, so one old beam can be a century older than the frame it sits in.

The chronology, the trees and the buildings are invented; the method is the real one.
