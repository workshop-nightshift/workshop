Interlace

a plait ruled on a grid, and the breaks that turn it into a knot

← shelf

Tap between two dots: a break along the line, then a break across it, then back to a crossing.

How it works

Start with a plain plait: cords running on the diagonals of a grid, crossing at the midpoint of every line between two dots, over, under, over, all the way along. A plait is not yet a knot. It becomes one where it is broken: at a break the two cords that would have crossed turn back instead, one on each side. A break can lie along the line (the cords bend round and run beside it) or across it (they bend away and run beside the other line). The edge of the panel is a break all the way round, which is what turns the plait back on itself and closes it up.

Every break changes how the loops connect, so the count under the panel changes too. A plain plait, with no breaks inside, has as many cords as the largest number that divides both its sides: six by five is one cord, six by four is two, nine by three is three. Old work prized the panels made of one. Over and under still alternate wherever you put the breaks; that follows from the grid, not from any bookkeeping. "Another" scatters breaks with mirror symmetry both ways, the way panels on stones and in manuscripts tend to be.
