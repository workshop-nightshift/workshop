Fog Line

A day of fog, and no one is sure where she is. Stand in for the land with the lead going.

    How a line of soundings fixes a ship

    Before radio fixes, a ship that couldn't see the land could still feel for it. She steers a steady course, and at every two cables run the leadsman heaves the lead and calls the depth. The lead's hollow foot is armed with tallow, and what sticks to it says what the bottom is: sand, mud, shell, gravel, or nothing at all off rock.

    The navigator pricks the soundings on a strip of tracing paper, spaced at the distance run and ruled along the course, with no idea yet where on the chart they belong. Then the paper goes over the chart, kept pointing the same way, and is slid about until its numbers sit on the chart's numbers. Where they agree, she is there.

    A hand lead only reaches twenty fathoms or so; deeper than that the call is no bottom, written as the depth with a bar over it. That's worth something too. Each dot on the tracing is tinted the chart's colour for its depth, so you can see where a cast sits in the wrong band.

    More casts make a surer fix, but she's standing in toward the land all the while. The seabed, coast and track are made up afresh for every fog; the soundings carry a little error, as real ones do.
